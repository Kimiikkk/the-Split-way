THE SHORT VERSION

Two halves. One key press. Follow the whole route.

A split board can look like one keyboard with the middle taken out. Electrically, it is usually two switch matrices, one or two controllers, and a link that lets both halves act as one. Press a key and a small chain begins: a switch closes, its diode keeps current on the intended path, the controller identifies a row and column, and firmware turns that position into a letter or action. Once you can follow that chain, every part has a job—and every compatibility check makes sense.

THE BOARD

PCB or hand wire

A PCB lays out the rows, columns, diode pads, and controller connections in copper. It makes a build repeatable, but its revision and footprints still matter. Hand wiring makes the same circuit with individual wires: more freedom for unusual shapes, more planning and more chances for a loose connection to hide. Neither approach changes the matrix logic; they change how you build and inspect it.

THE LINK

One cable, or none

A split needs a way for the halves to share key positions. A wired design may use serial or another documented transport over a cable; a wireless design may use Bluetooth Low Energy. The connector alone does not tell you the protocol: some TRRS cables also carry power, so their pinout and hot-plug behavior matter. Choose firmware and controllers around the board’s actual link, not around the cable you happen to own.

THE BODY

Plate, case, tent

The plate keeps switches aligned. The case supports the plate and PCB, leaves room for the controller, and sets the keyboard’s height and sound. Feet or a tenting wedge change how the halves meet your hands. A flat build is easiest to assemble and diagnose; temporary wedges let you test an angle before printing or buying a fixed case. A product photo can show the shape, but only a dry fit can tell you how it meets your hands.

BEFORE YOU BUY

Choose a shape your hands can return to

Layout is a reach map. Look at where your fingers rest, how far your thumbs travel, and which keys you are willing to move onto layers. Fewer keys can make a board smaller, but it asks firmware to carry more of the layout.

Measure your own hands. Popularity is not a fit test. Draw the key spacing on paper, mark where your fingers actually rest, and cut out a quick thumb-cluster mock-up. WIRED SPLIT

Simple, steady, easy to inspect

Usually two controllers with a physical cable between halves. A TRRS interconnect is common; some builds use USB-C serial or another wired link. Unplug USB before connecting or disconnecting TRRS unless the board documentation explicitly says it is safe. TRRS jacks can momentarily short contacts while plugging.

  • One half connects to the computer; it acts as the master.
  • The other half sends its matrix state across the interconnect.
  • Typically no batteries or radio pairing to manage.
WIRELESS SPLIT

More placement freedom, more power decisions

A BLE split has central and peripheral roles. In ZMK, the central receives key positions, runs the keymap, and talks to the computer; the peripheral sends its events to that central. Those roles affect firmware, pairing, reset, and power use. Plan the battery and charging path from the controller board’s documentation, and check sleep and wake behavior before closing the case.

  • Check the controller’s exact BLE support, pinout, and battery input.
  • Confirm both halves are supported by the same firmware shield.
  • Do not solder a cell directly to a bare MCU board unless its design specifies it.
THE BRAIN

Controllers: choose by job

The MCU is the chip; the controller is the small board you install. That board also determines the USB connector, pin labels, regulator, bootloader, and often the battery circuit. Two controllers can use the same MCU and still differ in the details that decide whether they fit your PCB and firmware.

Less memory and feature headroom than newer chips. Clones may differ in USB hardware, voltage, and bootloader even when the footprint looks familiar.

RP2040
Pi Pico class

A popular base for modern wired controllers, with more memory headroom than classic AVR boards. Compact RP2040 boards often copy a Pro Micro footprint, but the pin map and bootloader are still board-specific.

Check the board definition, USB boot procedure, and supported split transport before buying; a matching footprint is not a matching firmware target.

WIRED · QMK / KMK

nRF52840
BLE boards

A common choice for BLE split controllers, especially with ZMK. The chip’s radio is only part of the decision; the board must expose the pins your PCB needs and support your chosen firmware target.

Check pin mapping, regulator, charging circuit, USB behavior, and firmware support on the exact board. The name nRF52840 alone does not guarantee a drop-in replacement.

WIRELESS · ZMK

STM32 family
Many variants

A broad family used in many custom controller boards. Different STM32 parts have different cores, memory, peripherals, and firmware support.

“STM32” is not a pinout or a firmware target. Check the exact part number, controller board, bootloader, and transport support.

CHECK THE BOARD

Compatibility checklist Before ordering, confirm the PCB footprint; voltage and pin map; bootloader and flash method; firmware board target; split transport and pins; and, for wireless, the battery input and charging circuit. QMK’s generic split docs also call out MCU-family compatibility between halves. Check the exact firmware implementation rather than matching boards by footprint alone.

WHAT YOU FEEL

Switches, sockets & caps

A switch does not have a feel in isolation. Spring force, travel, stem wobble, lubrication, keycap weight, plate stiffness, case, and the way you strike a key all change what reaches your fingers and ears. Decide what you want to feel first; then check which parts can fit together.

Three switch families

M

Mechanical
A stem moves until metal contacts close. Linear models have no deliberate tactile bump; tactile models add one; clicky models add a distinct sound mechanism. Force curves and travel vary by switch, so a category or color name cannot tell you exactly how it will feel. Try samples if you can, and compare them at the speed and force you actually type.

L

Low profile
Low-profile switches shorten the housing and often the travel, which can lower the overall board. The label covers multiple systems: Kailh Choc and MX low-profile parts do not automatically share footprints, plates, or caps. Match the exact switch family to the PCB and keycap standard.

C

Kailh Choc family
Kailh Choc is one low-profile switch family, not a synonym for every compact switch. Choc versions and keycap stems can differ. Check the exact switch, PCB footprint, plate opening, and cap compatibility together before ordering.

Switch sensing & fit

Most DIY split boards use a mechanical switch whose metal contacts close when pressed. Optical switches use a light path, Hall effect switches measure a magnet’s position, and capacitive designs sense a change in capacitance. These technologies need compatible PCBs and firmware; a switch that fits the plate may still be electrically incompatible.

A force number in a listing is measured at a point in the press; it may describe actuation force or bottom-out force, and those are not the same. Compare like with like. Spring shape, lubrication, wobble, travel, plate, and cap mass all change the sensation. If the listing does not say where force was measured, treat the number as incomplete—not as a precise prediction of feel.

3-pin and 5-pin MX switches share two electrical contacts. The extra plastic posts on a 5-pin switch help locate it in a PCB. A plate can also locate a 3-pin switch on a 5-pin PCB; a board without locating holes may need those posts clipped. Check the PCB and switch guidance before cutting anything. Pin count says nothing about low-profile compatibility.

Mounting is part of the plan

Soldered switches are direct and secure, but difficult to replace. Hot-swap sockets allow switch changes without desoldering; the PCB must support the exact socket footprint and the switch pins must be straight. Choc sockets and MX sockets are different.

Do not force a switch into a hot-swap board. Seat the socket first, align both metal pins, then press straight down. A folded pin can miss the socket and damage it.

KEYCAP FIT

Stem, profile, material

Start at the switch stem: an MX cross stem accepts MX-style caps, while low-profile systems need their matching standard. Then check row profile, spacing, and unusual thumb sizes. Sculpted sets give rows different heights and angles; uniform sets keep a consistent shape, which can make moving keys between layers simpler. Cherry and OEM are common sculpted profiles; DSA and XDA are uniform; SA and MT3 are taller sculpted families. Names describe a family, not an exact height—check the maker’s measurements.

ABS is easy to mold with crisp legends and often feels smooth; it can develop a polished shine. PBT is generally more resistant to that shine and often has a textured surface, though thickness, sound, and quality vary. Double-shot legends are formed from two plastics; dye-sub legends are printed into the cap surface. Neither process guarantees a good fit or feel.

Many split layouts use mostly 1u keys plus several small thumb keys. A full-size set may contain the right letters but too few 1u caps, or no cap for a wider thumb key. Compare the kit’s physical layout with the set’s included sizes—not just the printed keymap—before you buy.

FIRST BUILD RECOMMENDATION

If you want to experiment with feel, an MX hot-swap board can make switch changes reversible—but only within the family supported by its sockets and PCB. For a low-profile build, choose the exact switch family first; then find a matching PCB, plate, and caps. Try one sample before committing to a full set.

THE CIRCUIT

Matrix, rows, columns & diodes

A matrix lets a modest number of controller pins read many switches. Diodes isolate each switch so combinations of held keys do not create unintended current paths.

ONE SWITCH + ONE DIODE AT EACH CROSSING

Why the diodes matter

Imagine holding three corners of a rectangle in the matrix. Without isolation, current can travel through more than one switch and make the fourth corner look pressed: a ghost key. A diode at each switch blocks that back path. The stripe marks the cathode, but the correct direction depends on the PCB and firmware scan design—follow the board’s own mark, not a photo of another keyboard.

The diode’s cathode stripe identifies its orientation. It must match the PCB markings or build guide. Reversing every diode is a common, consistent error—and a single reversed diode can make one key behave incorrectly.

—|>—Follow the board’s diode mark and schematic.
Never infer orientation from an unrelated keyboard photo.
Rows × columns

For 24 switches arranged as 4 rows by 6 columns, the controller scans four row wires and six column wires: ten GPIO pins rather than one pin per key. Add the pins needed for the inter-half link, reset, encoders, or other features. The physical layout and matrix map are related, but not identical; firmware has to describe the PCB’s actual rows and columns.

Direct pins

Very small boards may wire each switch to its own controller pin and ground. It is easy to reason about, but consumes more GPIO and still needs a clear grounding plan.

PCB vs hand-wire

For hand wiring, keep row and column buses insulated from each other and mechanically secure. Label the halves and photograph the wiring before closing the case.

ON THE BENCH

Tools & consumables

A soldering iron is only one part of a safe, repeatable setup. Good light, a stable board, suitable solder and flux, and fume extraction make the work easier to inspect and mistakes easier to avoid.

01

Temperature-controlled iron
A small chisel tip transfers heat to pad and lead more reliably than a needle point. Start with the solder and board maker’s guidance. If a joint takes too long, improve contact and tip condition before raising the temperature blindly.

02

Solder & flux
Use electronics-grade solder and compatible flux. Lead-free alloys usually need a higher working temperature and are less forgiving of poor technique. Extract fumes, keep food away, wash your hands after handling solder, and follow local rules for lead-containing materials.

03

Flush cutters & tweezers
Cut diode and switch leads close to the joint without pulling the pad. Tweezers control small parts; eye protection catches clipped leads.

04

Multimeter
Continuity mode checks paths and helps find a short between supply and ground while the board is unpowered. Use the schematic to choose test points; do not probe a powered board until you understand its rails and the meter’s mode.

05

Magnification & light
A bright bench lamp and loupe expose bridges, cold joints, and reversed diodes before the case hides them.

06

Switch tester (optional)
A few sample switches let you compare spring force, tactility, and sound before buying a full set.

ASSEMBLY

Make one good joint
at a time.

Before the iron is hot, read the board-specific build guide all the way through. Mark diode direction, identify every footprint, find the controller’s USB edge, and trace the interconnect and power path in the schematic. Check whether both halves may be powered at once. These details are not universal; checking now is easier than changing them after soldering.

THE JOINT

Heat the pad and lead together.

A good joint starts by heating the pad and component lead together, then feeding solder into those hot surfaces. Solder should flow and wet the metal; it is not a blob balanced on the iron or a bead hiding a cold connection. Let the joint cool without moving the part, then inspect it before trimming or moving on.

  1. Clean the tip and apply a small fresh film of solder so it transfers heat well.
  2. Place the tip against the pad and lead at the same time.
  3. Feed a little solder into the joint after both surfaces are hot; add only enough to wet them.
  4. When solder flows around the lead and pad, remove the solder wire first, then the iron.
  5. Hold the part still until the joint solidifies. Check for a smooth fillet, visible pad edge, and no bridge to a neighbor.
A sound jointWets both surfaces · covers the pad neatly · has no ball or bridge · does not move when cool.If solder beads upStop. Check for a dirty or oxidized surface, a poorly tinned tip, or missing flux. Do not keep heating the same pad indefinitely; let it cool before correcting the cause and trying again.
01

Inventory & dry fit

Lay out the left and right parts separately and compare them with the kit list. Check PCB revision, diode count, switch footprint, socket type, controller footprint, and connector orientation. Dry-fit plate and case. Make sure the controller can be inserted, reset, and removed before nearby parts make access difficult.

Check: every footprint has a matching part. MX and low-profile hardware are not interchangeable.

02

Fit and solder diodes

Place each diode with its cathode stripe aligned to the PCB mark. Tack one lead, then check that the body is seated and the stripe still points the right way before soldering the second lead. Work in small groups so an orientation mistake is easy to find. Trim only after both joints are cool, and wear eye protection: clipped leads can spring away.

Inspect: shiny, smooth fillets; no loose component; no solder bridge between pads.

03

Add sockets or switches

Hot-swap sockets are soldered to the PCB once; switches can then be changed without heating the board. Keep each socket flush with its pads: tack one pad, inspect the alignment, then solder the second. For direct-solder builds, hold switches in the plate so their pins meet the PCB holes squarely. A bent pin can look seated while missing its electrical contact.

Take care: a crooked socket or bent switch pin creates an intermittent key.

04

Install the controllers

Check the controller’s orientation against the PCB labels and case opening. If it uses sockets, solder the headers to the controller first, let them cool, then press it into the sockets without forcing it. Solder header pins to their pads; do not try to heat the MCU chip or USB connector. Keep reset and bootloader access reachable.

Verify: left/right orientation, reset access, pin labels, and correct controller target.

05

Wire the halves

Wire the halves from the schematic, not from connector shape or wire color. Confirm which contacts carry ground, power, and signals; check both ends for the same pin order. A TRRS cable can carry power, so disconnect USB and battery power before plugging or unplugging unless the exact design documents safe hot-plugging. A momentary short here can destroy a controller.

Before power: continuity-check ground and supply for an accidental short.

06

Test before closing

Test before the case hides anything. With power disconnected, check for a short between supply and ground. Then test one half at a time using the board’s firmware procedure; verify every switch, not just a few letters. Check reset and bootloader access. Only then power down, connect the halves as documented, and test the full matrix, handedness, and extra controls.

Tip: test with tweezers across hot-swap contacts to separate switch faults from circuit faults.

07

Close, route, and type

Route wires away from screw posts, sharp edges, switch pins, and the case seam. Do not trap or puncture a battery lead. Close the case loosely first, then reopen it and inspect the wire path before tightening. Live with the keyboard for a few sessions before adding fixed tenting or wrist support; small changes are easier to judge than a permanent guess.

Finish: save the working firmware files, pinout, and build notes with the keyboard.

MAKE IT YOURS

Firmware & the first boot

Firmware turns physical positions into keycodes, layers, and behavior. Choose it before the controller: board support, split transport, and flashing workflow determine which hardware will be straightforward to build.

QMK

Feature-rich wired workhorse

QMK is a flexible choice for many wired splits and feature-rich keymaps. A working build still needs the right keyboard definition, matrix, transport, and handedness setup. QMK’s generic split guidance has MCU-family and transport constraints; a custom board may use a different implementation. Start with the target for your exact keyboard, then confirm both controllers are supported by it.

Split keyboard configuration ↗
ZMK

Wireless-first, BLE capable

ZMK is a common choice for BLE splits. The central receives positions from peripheral halves, applies the keymap, and connects to the host; roles and build targets are part of the configuration. Check the board or shield guide for pairing, reset, and flashing. A wireless build also needs a documented battery, switch, and charging plan—not merely a radio-capable chip.

ZMK documentation ↗
KMK

Python-oriented alternative

KMK runs on supported CircuitPython boards and documents wired UART split setups. Its documentation also describes Bluetooth split support as still in testing, so check the current status before choosing it for a wireless build. Confirm the controller, CircuitPython version, pins, and split options together.

KMK split guide ↗
First boot, in orderBring the board up in stages. Keep the halves separate until each one can be flashed and tested on its own.
  1. Flash the documented target to one half and confirm its bootloader and USB connection behave as expected.
  2. Run the firmware’s key test. Check every switch, the reset sequence, and the positions reported by the matrix.
  3. Repeat for the other half. Apply the correct side or handedness setting; do not assume both controller images are interchangeable.
  4. Disconnect power before attaching the interconnect. Use only the cable and pinout specified for this board.
  5. Power up and test keys on both halves. Then test layers, encoders, displays, and other features one at a time.
  6. Save the configuration, firmware files, board revision, and pinout with your build notes. A working build is easier to repair when its setup is written down.
WHEN SOMETHING IS OFF

Find the fault in layers

Troubleshoot from the outside in. First ask what the computer sees, then check firmware and the inter-half link, then trace the matrix, and finally inspect the switch and its joints. Change one thing at a time so the result tells you something.

One key does nothing+

If the board is hot-swap, power it down and reseat the switch; check that both pins are straight. Bridge the socket contacts with tweezers only if the board’s guide describes that test. If the key registers, inspect the switch and socket. If not, check that key’s diode direction, solder joints, and row/column path against the schematic.

A whole row or column is dead+

Several keys failing together points to something they share: a bus, wire, controller pin, or firmware mapping. Disconnect power, then test continuity along that row or column from the controller outward. Compare firmware matrix order with the PCB definition before touching individual switches.

Keys appear on the wrong side+

Check side configuration first: handedness, left/right firmware targets, and controller orientation. Then compare the physical matrix order with the keyboard definition. QMK and ZMK handle roles differently; use the instructions for the firmware and board you actually built.

Connecting the halves resets or kills the board+

Disconnect USB and battery power immediately. Do not reconnect to see if it happens again. Check cable type and contact order, jack soldering, and any short between supply and ground against the schematic. Replace a suspect cable only with the specified wiring; connector fit does not prove pinout compatibility.

Wireless halves will not pair+

Check that one build is central and the other peripheral, both use the same shield configuration, and the documented pairing/reset sequence has been followed. Confirm each half is powered and its switch is on. If the board supports battery reporting, verify the battery before changing firmware settings.

The controller will not flash+

Confirm the exact controller model and firmware target, then enter its bootloader using the documented reset sequence. Try a known data-capable USB cable and check whether the board appears to the operating system. A shared footprint does not mean a shared bootloader; do not flash a file meant for a visually similar board.

A NOTE FOR THE NEXT BUILD

Write down what your hands teach you.

After a week, write down what your hands have learned: keys you reach for, thumb positions that feel awkward, and layers you forget. Change one thing at a time, then give it a few sessions. The useful build is not the one with the most unusual parts; it is the one whose layout lets you get on with your work—and whose notes make the next repair possible.
